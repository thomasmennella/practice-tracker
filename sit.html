Recognizing Concentration Depth — Cross-Reference Guide ▾

Use this after a sit to cross-reference what you experienced. Not a checklist to consult during practice.

Momentary concentration

Attention lands and wanders repeatedly. You keep returning. No sustained hold. This is the ordinary starting point — nothing is wrong.

Access concentration (upacāra-samādhi)

Hindrances have quieted but not vanished. Mind settles but remains aware of sounds, body, surroundings. Attention stays on the breath for extended stretches without effort. A sign or light impression of the breath may appear. You could be interrupted and return. The mind is collected but not absorbed.

First absorption (first jhāna) — 4 factors present together

  • Applied attention (vitakka) — You actively place attention on the breath. Like pressing a piano key. You notice yourself choosing to return.
  • Sustained attention (vicāra) — Attention stays without constant re-application. Like holding the key down. Breath holds you for multiple cycles without effort.
  • Rapture (pīti) — Physical pleasurable energy: tingles, goosebumps, waves up the spine, involuntary smiling, tears of joy, body feeling electrically alive. Unmistakable when strong. You did nothing to create it.
  • Pleasure (sukha) — Calm, sweet, settled happiness. Softer than rapture. Like sinking into a warm bath. Zero desire to move or stop. Sitting feels profoundly pleasant.

All four present together, sustained 10+ minutes. Breath becomes subtle, may nearly disappear. Time distortion common. Hindrances completely suppressed — not reduced, gone.

Second absorption — 3 factors

Applied and sustained attention drop away — you are no longer "doing" anything. Rapture and pleasure merge into stable, unified joy. Less fizzy, more like rapture dissolved into thick honey. One-pointedness (ekaggatā): attention locked — not watching the breath, you are the breath experience. No meditator watching. Effort has completely ceased.

Third absorption — 2 factors

Rapture is gone. What remains: exquisite ease without excitement — like floating in space. Equanimity (upekkhā) and one-pointedness only. Perfect comfort. You could stay indefinitely. Breathing may become imperceptible.

Fourth absorption — 2 factors

Even pleasure is gone. Pure, neutral balance. Clear, luminous awareness. Nothing pleasant, nothing unpleasant — pure equanimity and one-pointedness. Breathing typically stops or becomes undetectable. Profound stillness and purity.

Honest cross-reference: Brief rapture or pleasant energy during a sit is not first absorption. Threshold means all four factors are locked together in stable unity for 10+ minutes, with hindrances fully absent. When in doubt, you have not crossed the threshold — and that is fine.

The Three Signs (Nimitta) — A Guide ▾

As concentration deepens in breath meditation, a mental image of the breath tends to arise on its own. It passes through three stages. Recognition is for after the sit.

Preparatory sign (parikamma nimitta)

A faint, unstable image or impression — fuzzy, dim, flickering. No fixed shape. Depends entirely on the physical breath; dissolves when attention wavers. You are still holding it together. This is the ordinary, correct starting point.

Acquired sign (uggaha nimitta)

The same image, now steadied. It holds its shape clearly and no longer requires the physical breath to remain — the breath can recede while the sign persists. You are no longer feeding it effortfully. The steadiness is usually unmistakable; the absence of doubt is itself part of the marker.

Counterpart sign (paṭibhāga nimitta)

The image becomes luminous and fully stable. The mind sinks into it and rests there without strain or maintenance. This is the doorway to access concentration and, beyond it, absorption.

Five Hindrances
Five Hindrances (Pañca Nīvaraṇā) — A Guide ▾

Nīvaraṇa means obstruction, covering. The definition is functional, not dramatic: a hindrance is anything that obstructs the mind's collectedness and weakens paññā. The criterion is not intensity — it is whether it altered your relationship to the object.

The canonical similes — the monkey, the man in debt, the boiling water — are vivid because they teach recognition to someone who has never introspected. They are the loud end of a spectrum, not diagnostic thresholds. Scoring against them trains blindness exactly where practice lives: it is the faint ripple, not the storm, that stands between access and jhāna.

The three-step test — apply in order:
1. Did attention move off the object? If no → None.
2. Did it return by itself within a breath or two? If yes → None. Ordinary mind-wandering, not a hindrance.
3. Did it take effort to return, or recur, or colour the sit? If yes → log it.
The question is not "was it severe?" but "did it obstruct?"

The tiers below map directly to the buttons: None = below threshold · Minor = at the boundary · Major = full-blown.

1. Sensual desire (kāmacchanda)

The mind leaning toward pleasant experience — an image, a plan, a taste, a comfort. Not the arising of the pleasant thing; the pull toward it.

None: A flicker of a pleasant image that passes without the mind leaning. Noticing a comfortable posture.
Minor: The mind leans and dwells — a plan, a meal, a purchase — with faint sweetness and reluctance to leave it. Repeated small adjustments for comfort. Also: savouring pīti, holding it as a possession rather than resting in it as an object. That is kāmacchanda operating inside jhāna territory, easily missed because it is dressed as attainment.
Major: Extended fantasy — acquisitive, erotic, elaborated. You come to ten minutes later having been fully inside a scenario.

2. Ill will (byāpāda)

Aversion with a target — the push-away to desire's pull. Any strength counts.

None: A brief flash of irritation at a noise that does not persist.
Minor: Low-grade aversion with something specific in view — an ache, a sound, a person from the day. Impatience with the sit. Irritation that the nimitta isn't stabilising. Faint self-criticism: self-directed ill will is still byāpāda, and it is the form most often left unnamed, because it arrives disguised as standards.
Major: Rehearsing an argument. Heat in the chest. Anger sustained over minutes.

3. Sloth and torpor (thīna-middha)

Thīna is sluggishness of mind, middha heaviness of body. A dimming of the object, not a calming of it.

None: One yawn. A moment of soft focus that resolves on its own.
Minor: The object is there but dull — the breath findable, but with no definition, texture or interest. Attention passive, drifting, sticky. There is a subtle pleasantness to it that makes you not want to fix it, which is what makes it insidious. Nodding once. Losing a breath and re-finding it without knowing where you went. A sit that felt calm but that you cannot remember much of is very often torpor, not samādhi. They are hard to tell apart from the inside; the diagnostic is brightness. Samādhi is bright, still and knowing. Torpor is dim, still and vague.
Major: Head dropping. Micro-sleeps. Losing minutes.

A tired pre-sit state is the nutriment. When it is present, assume torpor is in the room and go looking, rather than waiting for it to announce itself.

4. Restlessness and worry (uddhacca-kukkucca)

Two distinct phenomena bundled because they share a nutriment. Uddhacca is unsettledness and scatter; kukkucca is remorse or worry over an open loop. Note in the detail box which of the two it was.

None: A thought arises and passes; you return. A task is remembered, noted, released.
Minor — uddhacca: Proliferation, one thought breeding the next, without emotional charge. Planning, reviewing. And in its most refined form: subtle striving. Leaning forward into the sit. Checking whether the nimitta has come. Assessing how the sit is going while it is going — that is uddhacca, and it disguises itself as diligence.
Minor — kukkucca: A specific open loop with a real-world answer you cannot reach from the cushion — "did I do that? I'm not sure" — that the mind keeps returning to and that casts a shadow over the object.
Major: Unable to hold the object for three breaths together; or spiralling worry with body activation and complete loss of the object.

5. Doubt (vicikicchā)

Uncertainty that stalls rather than investigates. Inquiry keeps practising while it looks; doubt stops.

None: A passing technical question about method.
Minor: "Am I doing this right?" "Is this a real nimitta or am I making it up?" "Is this actually first jhāna?" "Should I be doing vipassanā instead?" Mid-sit second-guessing of technique. Nearly invisible, because it masquerades as diligence and as scholarship.
Major: "I don't know if this works at all. I don't know if I'm capable of it." Sustained loss of confidence that stops practice.

Certainty is not the absence of doubt — it is doubt's other face. Settling the question by declaration is the same movement in either direction.

A hindrance-free sit is a real category — in absorption the five are genuinely suppressed; that is what jhāna is — but it is rarer than most logs claim. Absorption occupies only part of a sit. The first eight minutes are where the hindrances live, and that is usually what goes unrecorded. Recording them is not bookkeeping: dhammānupassanā begins with knowing the hindrance present, absent, arising, abandoned, and not recurring. Handled this way they are not interruptions of the practice — they are the object of it.
None — attention stayed, or returned by itself within a breath or two. Minor — it took effort to return, or it recurred, or it coloured the sit. Major — the object was lost.

Insight & Phenomena
Insight Knowledges & Phenomena — A Guide ▾

These are not stages to pursue — they are what the mind sees when conditions are sufficiently established. Log what was genuinely present, not what you hope was present.

Three characteristics

Anicca — Directly perceiving the arising and passing of phenomena moment to moment. Not the concept of impermanence but seeing it happen.
Dukkha — The pressured, unsatisfactory quality of conditioned experience — the subtle stress underlying even pleasant states.
Anattā — The absence of a controller behind what is happening. Attention moves without a "mover." Particularly striking when noticed without seeking it.

Insight knowledges (vipassanā-ñāṇa)

Arising & passing (udayabbaya) — Rapid, clear perception of phenomena arising and ceasing. May arrive with unusual clarity or energy. A significant marker.
Dissolution (bhaṅga) — Only the passing side is prominent; phenomena vanish as soon as they appear.
Fearfulness / danger / disenchantment — The natural response to clearly seeing impermanence. Not depression — a lucid seeing of the instability of formations.
Equanimity toward formations — Deep, balanced stillness. Neither grasping nor pushing away. The most mature pre-path insight knowledge.

Unusual phenomena

Luminosity, body movements, surges of rapture (pīti), spatial distortion, cessation of breath, or profound stillness may arise. These are byproducts of deepening practice — not goals, not problems. Note them without amplifying them.

The ten corruptions of insight (vipassanūpakkilesa) — illumination, rapture, bliss, and others — can be mistaken for attainment. Their arising is not the problem; attachment to them is. A teacher's guidance is most valuable precisely here.
